Question 1

Do discounted orders actually sell more product?

Why it matters

Discounts cost Northwind $88,666 — 6.5% of gross sales. If discounts don't move more product, that is margin given away for nothing.

Approach

A simple discounted-vs-not comparison is misleading if popular products are discounted more often. So each product is compared only with itself: its average units per order line when discounted versus when sold at full price. All 77 products were sold both ways, giving 77 paired comparisons (838 discounted lines out of 2,155). Differences are tested with a paired t-test and a Wilcoxon signed-rank test, then repeated at each discount level.

Main finding

Yes — discounted lines sell more product. The typical product moves 25% more units per order line when discounted (paired t-test p < 0.001, Wilcoxon p < 0.001). But the extra volume only roughly offsets the lower price: net revenue per line is $614 discounted vs. $581 at full price, a difference that is not statistically significant (p = 0.376). The lift also plateaus: 15%, 20% and 25% discounts all produce a similar 13–18% increase, while the 10% tier shows no lift at all.

Business implication

Treat discounts as a tool for moving units (e.g. clearing stock), not for growing revenue. Since 20–25% discounts move no more volume than 15%, cap routine discounts at 15% and require sign-off above it. Review how the 10% tier is used — it gives up price with no measurable volume gain.

Caveat: this is association, not causation — reps may grant discounts because a customer is already placing a large order. Some products have only one or two lines on one side, so individual dots are noisy; the conclusion rests on the paired test across all products.
