Discounts, sales reps, and product bundles at Northwind

Three questions a sales manager should be able to answer from Northwind's 830 orders and 2,155 order lines: whether discounts earn their cost, where sales performance really differs, and which products customers buy together.

  1. Do discounted orders actually sell more product?
  2. How does performance vary among the sales representatives?

Question 1

Do discounted orders actually sell more product?

Why it matters

Discounts cost Northwind $88,666 — 6.5% of gross sales. If discounts don't move more product, that is margin given away for nothing.

Approach

A simple discounted-vs-not comparison is misleading if popular products are discounted more often. So each product is compared only with itself: its average units per order line when discounted versus when sold at full price. All 77 products were sold both ways, giving 77 paired comparisons (838 discounted lines out of 2,155). Differences are tested with a paired t-test and a Wilcoxon signed-rank test, then repeated at each discount level.

Main finding

Yes — discounted lines sell more product. The typical product moves 25% more units per order line when discounted (paired t-test p < 0.001, Wilcoxon p < 0.001). But the extra volume only roughly offsets the lower price: net revenue per line is $614 discounted vs. $581 at full price, a difference that is not statistically significant (p = 0.376). The lift also plateaus: 15%, 20% and 25% discounts all produce a similar 13–18% increase, while the 10% tier shows no lift at all.

Business implication

Treat discounts as a tool for moving units (e.g. clearing stock), not for growing revenue. Since 20–25% discounts move no more volume than 15%, cap routine discounts at 15% and require sign-off above it. Review how the 10% tier is used — it gives up price with no measurable volume gain.

Caveat: this is association, not causation — reps may grant discounts because a customer is already placing a large order. Some products have only one or two lines on one side, so individual dots are noisy; the conclusion rests on the paired test across all products.

Question 2

How does performance vary among the sales representatives?

Why it matters

Knowing whether top performers win through more deals or bigger deals tells a manager what to coach, where to add headcount, and which reps need attention.

Approach

For each of the 9 employees who took orders: net revenue (after discounts), orders, distinct customers, average order value, discount rate given, and the share of orders shipped after the required date. A Kruskal-Wallis test checks whether order values differ between reps. Growth is compared as monthly revenue because the data covers only 6 months of 1996 and 5 of 1998.

Main finding

Revenue differences between reps come from how many orders and customers they handle, not deal size. Margaret Peacock leads with $232,891 (18.4%) from 156 orders, while average order values are statistically indistinguishable across reps. Most reps roughly doubled their monthly revenue from 1997 to 1998, but Michael Suyama and Steven Buchanan stayed flat. Robert King gives the deepest discounts (11.8%) and Anne Dodsworth has the highest late-shipment rate (9.3%).

Business implication

Focus coaching and territory planning on customer coverage and order frequency rather than upselling. The UK office's lower totals partly reflect that two of its four people are managers — review its capacity before judging individuals. Follow up specifically on Suyama's and Buchanan's flat growth, King's discounting, and Dodsworth's late shipments.

Caveat: Andrew Fuller (VP) and Steven Buchanan (manager) also carry management duties, and customer assignments are not random, so raw totals are not a pure measure of individual skill.

Question 3

Which products tend to be bundled or bought together?

Why it matters

Products that reliably appear together are candidates for bundles, cross-sell prompts, and coordinated stocking.

Approach

Market-basket analysis over 830 orders: for every product pair seen together in at least 5 orders, compute lift (how much more often they co-occur than if bought independently) and confidence. A one-sided Fisher exact test checks each pair, with a Bonferroni correction for the 1,535 pairs tested. The same lift is computed between product categories.

Main finding

Only one pairing is a reliable bundle: Sir Rodney's Scones and Sirop d'érable appear together in 8 orders — 7.1× more than chance, and 33% of maple-syrup orders also include the scones (Fisher p < 0.001). Several cheese and dessert pairs (e.g. Gorgonzola Telino with Pavlova or Mozzarella) are 2–3× chance but rest on only 5–7 orders. At the category level every pair sits below 1.0: customers place focused orders rather than mixed baskets.

Business implication

Launch a scones-and-syrup bundle or cross-sell prompt — the evidence supports it. Treat the cheese/dessert pairs as hypotheses to test with a small promotion rather than as established patterns. Because orders are small and focused, broad cross-category bundling is unlikely to pay off.

Caveat: with 77 products and a median of 2 items per order, most pairs are rare, so lift estimates for low-count pairs are unstable.
