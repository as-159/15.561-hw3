Question 2

How does performance vary among the sales representatives?

Why it matters

Knowing whether top performers win through more deals or bigger deals tells a manager what to coach, where to add headcount, and which reps need attention.

Approach

For each of the 9 employees who took orders: net revenue (after discounts), orders, distinct customers, average order value, discount rate given, and the share of orders shipped after the required date. A t-test (Welch's, which allows unequal spreads) compares each rep's average order value with the average of all other reps' orders. Growth is compared as monthly revenue because the data covers only 6 months of 1996 and 5 of 1998.

Main finding

Revenue differences between reps come from how many orders and customers they handle, not deal size. Margaret Peacock leads with $232,891 (18.4%) from 156 orders, and for 7 of the 9 reps the t-test finds no significant difference between their average order value and the rest of the team's. The two exceptions write significantly smaller orders (Michael Suyama $1,103 vs. $1,562, p < 0.001; Laura Callahan $1,220 vs. $1,569, p = 0.006). Most reps roughly doubled their monthly revenue from 1997 to 1998, but Michael Suyama and Steven Buchanan stayed flat. Robert King gives the deepest discounts (11.8%) and Anne Dodsworth has the highest late-shipment rate (9.3%).

Business implication

Focus coaching and territory planning on customer coverage and order frequency rather than upselling. The UK office's lower totals partly reflect that two of its four people are managers — review its capacity before judging individuals. Follow up specifically on Suyama's and Buchanan's flat growth, why Suyama's and Callahan's orders run smaller, King's discounting, and Dodsworth's late shipments.

Caveat: Andrew Fuller (VP) and Steven Buchanan (manager) also carry management duties, and customer assignments are not random, so raw totals are not a pure measure of individual skill.
